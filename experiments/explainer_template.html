A walkthrough of what got built

One arm, two worlds, one question

A digital twin is normally reported as a success and a sim-to-real gap as a single number. Neither tells you anything useful. fieldtwin measures the part that matters: a twin is wrong in particular directions, and only some of them change what the robot does.

1 — the setup

A robot arm pushing a block

Everything happens in this scene. A 3-link arm pushes a block across a table. There is a pipe and a bolt in the scene so that later, when a vision-language model is asked to find something, it has to pick the right object instead of the only object.

The whole world is 6 numbers: three joint angles, plus the block's x, y and rotation. That is small enough to simulate thousands of times on a laptop, which is the entire reason the project is feasible in three months without a GPU.

2 — the idea

The plant and the twin

The plant

The ground truth. The only thing allowed to be called reality. You never touch it directly — every command goes through a simulated servo bus that is late, jittery and occasionally drops a packet, exactly like real hardware.

The twin

What you get after trying to identify the plant's parameters from logged data. It shares the same scene file, so the only possible difference is the 18 numbers describing mass, friction, damping and servo gain.

Because the plant is simulated too, its true parameters are known. That means every claim about the gap can itself be checked — which a real robot never allows. That is the reason to do this in simulation, not an apology for it.

4 — the interface

The wire is part of the physics

On real hardware you never command a joint, you command a bus. It applies your setpoint about 12 ms late, with jitter, sometimes loses a packet entirely, and reports position back on a finite grid. All of that arrives in your data looking like dynamics.

Already a result. With this bus running, a twin holding the plant's exactly correct parameters still cannot match the data better than 7.9× the sensor noise. Before a single parameter is mis-fitted, the interface alone sets the floor.

5 — identifiability

What the data cannot tell you

Fit the 18 parameters to logged data and look at the curvature of that fit. Directions with steep curvature are pinned down by the data. Flat directions are not — you could be badly wrong along them and the data would look the same.

Identification set

One direction is exactly flat, for a reason worth knowing. MuJoCo combines contact friction by taking the larger of the two surfaces. The fingertip's friction (0.45) sits below the block's (0.50), so it has no effect whatsoever. Not "hard to measure" — provably invisible. No amount of extra robot time would fix it, because the fix is in the contact model, not the data.

6 — what the walkthrough turned up

Adding more data made the answer worse

Building this page surfaced something the first run had hidden. Free-space sweeps, with the block parked away, identify the arm parameters extremely sharply. That inflates the largest eigenvalue by orders of magnitude — and because "flat" was defined relative to the largest, every contact parameter then falls below the line and gets labelled unidentifiable, despite the pushes constraining it perfectly well.

Same model, same noise, same 57% parameter error — only the identification set differs.

This sharpens the project rather than breaking it. Identifiability is a property of the model, the dataset and the noise assumptions together. A direction that is flat for your fitting loss can be exactly the direction your task is most sensitive to, because the loss is averaged over whatever episodes you happened to collect and the task is not. Two concrete consequences: the relative cutoff has to be replaced with one referenced to sensor noise, and the stiff/flat split has to be computed against a task-weighted metric.
